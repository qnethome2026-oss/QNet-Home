Use case 1 · urgency: safety

Somebody is on the floor. Now what?

A fall alone is not the emergency. Being on the floor for three hours because nobody knew is the emergency. This capability closes the gap between the two — it detects, it asks, it listens, and when it hears nothing it escalates on a clock, without waiting for a human to notice a notification.

Detection

A YOLO11n fine-tune for fall detection runs continuously on the room node's Hexagon NPU. The model is pre-compiled and committed to the repository as a QNN context binary for both boards, so reproducing the system needs no model-conversion account.

The gate: 5 of 8 frames

A single frame classified fallen means nothing — motion blur, a partial view, a person crouching to pick something up. The detector fires only when the class holds across 5 of the last 8 frames, above a confidence floor of 0.6. After firing it enters a hold-off state rather than re-arming immediately, so one fall produces one event.

Why 0.6, with receipts. The floor was probed live rather than guessed. Real fallen-person frames read 0.86–0.94. A photo of a fallen person held up to the camera read 0.40–0.52, intermittently. A blank wall with a floor of 0.35 active read up to 0.52 — pure noise.

Two conclusions were recorded: 0.6 is reaffirmed, because 0.35 fires on nothing at all; and screen-replay is not a viable demo trigger, because its signal sits in the same band as wall noise. One screen-replay event did fire during the low-floor window — and was excluded precisely because it was indistinguishable from noise.

What gets published

Never a frame. Never a clip. One JSON object on qnet/<room>/event: 

{ "id": "01JQ...", "ts": 1785790153.4, "room": "kitchen",
  "kind": "fall.detected", "conf": 0.87,
  "meta": { "cls": "Fallen", "frames": "5/8" } }

A stated limitation, not a hidden one. The model reads deliberate lying down as a fall. It classifies posture, not intent. Someone choosing to lie on the floor will trigger a session — which is why cancelling is always one word away, spoken or typed or clicked.

The escalation ladder

Every step below is engine-owned. The timers fire on schedule, the notifications send themselves, and the emergency call is placed automatically. The language model's entire contribution is deciding whether "I'm alright I think" means ok or escalate, and wording the reassurance in between.

  • T+0s · PHASE: check
    The room speaks first

    "I saw you fall. Take a breath — are you okay?" — a canned line, spoken instantly, with no model call. If the LLM is down, cold, or thinking, this line still happens. Then the node opens the microphone and listens.

  • ON "I'M FINE"
    One mandatory double-check

    "Any pain? Did you hit your head?" — canned, and unskippable. "I'm fine" can never close a session without passing through this. People minimise, especially to a machine, and a head strike is exactly the injury that gets talked away. Only a clear denial of pain closes the session.

  • T+30s · PHASE: escalate
    Trusted contacts are messaged automatically

    No answer, "no", or any report of pain moves here — and the timer moves here on its own if nothing is said at all. The Telegram fires on phase entry as an on_enter action, not as something the agent has to remember to do:

    🔴 Possible fall — Tony, kitchen. Reply OK if you can check on Tony — otherwise I'll call emergency services in 30 seconds.

    The room says what it did, out loud: "I've messaged Sarah." Not filler — an actual fact about the world.

  • IF A CONTACT REPLIES "OK" · PHASE: contact_engaged
    The call is held, and the person is told who is coming

    "Good news — Sarah saw my message and is coming to check on you. I'll stay with you until they arrive." The contact gets a confirmation too: "Got it — I'll hold off on emergency services."

    A 180-second backstop timer starts anyway. An acknowledged incident that then goes quiet must never be a stranded one — if nobody resolves it, the call still gets placed.

  • T+60s · PHASE: call_help
    Emergency services

    "I'm calling emergency services for you right now. Help is coming — stay with me." The contact is told: "📞 No response from Tony — calling emergency services now (kitchen)."

    A contact can also skip the whole ladder at any point by replying "call 911" — that fires the call immediately.

The numbers in the message and the numbers in the timer are the same numbers. The Telegram promises 30 seconds because escalate's timer is 30 seconds; the hold-off promises 3 minutes because contact_engaged's backstop is 180 seconds. Change one, change the other — the skill file says so explicitly, in the file, next to both.

Nobody is left in silence

The hardest part of this problem is not the escalation. It is the waiting. Between the Telegram and the ambulance there is a person on the floor, alone, and the system has to be present without being unbearable.

Three findings from testing on real people

A status line every 20 seconds reads as hovering, not care

The original design gave the agent a turn whenever ~20 seconds passed in silence. In practice that is relentless. The default became a schedule instead: a status update about a minute in, another at two minutes, then one every five. If something else just spoke — a phase opening, a reply, a responder brief — the scheduled slot is skipped outright, because that line already did the slot's only job.

A repeated sentence reads as a recording, not a companion

The facts in a comfort update stay identical, but consecutive updates must never share wording. The engine rotates through phrasings — "I'm still here with you…", "Still with you —…", "I haven't gone anywhere…" — around the same grounded facts.

"Help me" deserved better than the next scheduled status line

Mid-escalation utterances that matched no exit condition were falling through to silence. Now an unrouted utterance always gets a reply. If the person's words name a problem that skills/first-aid.md knows about, that topic's sourced sentence is spoken, wrapped in presence and the one promise the session has actually made. Otherwise a fallback: "I'm right here with you. Try to get comfortable and don't strain to move — help is on the way."

The model never words medical guidance. First-aid content comes from skills/first-aid.md, whose frontmatter cites its sources — IFRC 2020 First Aid Guidelines, AHA 2020 Highlights, and a California DSS/IHSS fall-response guide. The engine selects a topic and speaks that topic's sentence verbatim. A 2B model improvising medical advice to someone who has just fallen is not a risk worth any amount of naturalness.

And when speech itself is garbled

Whisper misheard "I'm still bleeding" as "I'm so leading" in a live test. The 2B model, prompted every which way, answered the garble with invented empathy — which is the worst possible response to a possible report of bleeding.

The fix: unclear became one of the classification options, and the rails speak a canned line — "Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?" The model flags. It never words.

Cancelling

A false alarm has to be trivially easy to stop, or people learn to dread the system. Three routes, all equivalent:

  • Say it — "cancel", "stop", "never mind", "false alarm". Matched on the engine rails as an interrupt, before the model ever sees the transcript.
  • Click it — the dashboard incident banner's Mark resolved.
  • Type it — from the admin portal.

Cancel matching uses word boundaries deliberately, so "stopped" and "cancelled the paper" don't close a session. And "I'm fine" is deliberately not a cancel phrase — it is a reply, and it goes through the pain double-check like every other reply.

If contacts were already notified, they get told: "✅ False alarm — Tony confirmed they're okay (kitchen). No action needed." Nobody is left driving over for an incident that resolved itself.

The first-responder brief

Someone arrives. They don't know what happened, and the person on the floor may not be able to tell them. They say:

"I'm the first responder — give me a summary of what happened."

And they get a spoken handover, grounded entirely in the session log: condition first, then what was done, then where things stand. The clinically relevant fact leads, because that is the order a responder needs it in.

This is an engine-level interrupt, not a session — it pauses the comfort loop, reads the room's latest fall file, words the timeline once, speaks it, and resumes. Because it is not a session, the one-session-per-room rule cannot block it, so a responder can always ask, even mid-escalation. The responder phrase is checked before the wake phrase, in any state. Ask in a room with no fall on file and the answer is exactly: "No fall has been recorded in this room."
