Reference numbers — Qualcomm AI Hub

The detector is a fine-tune of YOLO11-N (640×640, 2.64 M parameters). AI Hub publishes reference latency for the stock YOLOv11-Detection on the QCS8275 — the Ventuno Q class of part, and the only tier that runs this model, since fall detection happens on the room node and never on the hub:

ChipsetRuntimePrecisionInferencePeak memory
QCS8275 Ventuno Q classQNNfloat12.92 ms0–184 MB

That is the like-for-like reference: same runtime, same precision, same class of part as what we deploy — a FP32 QNN context binary, no calibration pass needed. Quantising to INT8 is the named next step, and has not been run on-device yet.

There is no published CPU figure to compare against. Every one of AI Hub's ~90 benchmark rows for this model — across three runtimes and four precisions — targets the NPU. So the CPU column below is our own measurement on the board, not a vendor number.

Why this matters for a room node, not just a benchmark. The CPU path saturates six of eight cores for the duration of every inference — on a board that is also running the VLM, Whisper, TTS and a camera pipeline, that is the difference between a system that works and one that thrashes. The NPU path leaves the CPU free, which is what makes four models coexist on one Ventuno Q.

Read honestly. N=20 with no thermal soak — enough to establish "the NPU wins and roughly by how much," not a paper. The power sensor reads the whole board's 12 V rail, so fan, Wi-Fi and background daemons are in every figure; it is the right number for "what does this cost the room node," not a silicon-level comparison. The NPU power average rests on only 11 samples because the run finished in 1.27 s. And both paths ran FP32 — this isolates which compute unit, not what precision.

Our 32.5 ms also sits above AI Hub's 12.92 ms reference: a different part (QCS8300 vs QCS8275), a fine-tuned checkpoint rather than the stock one, and unquantised weights.

The conversation loop — LLM and TTS

Detection is one model; the conversation is three more. Neither AI Hub nor any vendor publishes latency for a generative or speech-synthesis model in a form comparable to this deployment, so every number below is ours, measured on the boards.

StageModel · deviceMeasured
Hear Whisper-small · Ventuno Q HTP 700 ms VAD endpointing · silence returns {"silence": true}
Classify Gemma 4 E2B Q4_0 · IQ-9075 via GenieX 424–494 ms warm · 5 545 ms cold on the first call of a session
Word a line same ~1.8 s a reply · ~1.5 s a comfort update · 15.7–16.2 tok/s decode, 0.18 s TTFT warm
Speak Piper TTS · Ventuno Q 1.8–9.6 s per line (~9 chars/s) — 32 chars 1.81 s, 55 chars 3.12 s, 83 chars 9.57 s
Full turn heard → say, end to end median 2.37 s → 1.61 s, max 5.58 s → 1.64 s after the fix round

TTS is the bottleneck, and it costs more than time. The node is half-duplex by design — no echo cancellation needed, but it is deaf while it speaks. A long safety line plus a comfort line back to back measured a 15.2 s continuous deaf stretch, and one TTS runner hang left a node deaf and mute for ~30 s from second zero of a session. Barge-in is impossible by construction.

The cold-start classify was the other big win: a warm-up ping at session open removed 5.5 s from the first reply, taking it to 0.48 s.

Never a frame, never a clip, never an audio buffer. Everything carrying a decision or a person's words is QoS 1; only the 5-second health heartbeat is QoS 0, because the next one is along shortly.

5 · Step by step, what actually happens

  • STEP 1 · qnet/node/vision.py → qnet/<room>/event · QoS 1
    The NPU sees a fall and publishes an event

    YOLO11n scores every frame. Five of the last eight come back fallen above 0.6, so the node publishes fall.detected to the broker — and then holds off, so one fall is one event. No frame moves.

  • STEP 2 · qnet/agent/engine.py · phases.py loads skills/fall.md
    The agent opens a session and loads the skill

    The engine matches the event's kind to skills/fall.md, opens a session for that room, enters the first phase — check — and starts its 30-second timer immediately. From here the clock runs whatever anyone says.

  • STEP 3 · qnet/<room>/say · {"prio": "safety"}
    The room speaks, with no model call

    The phase's opening is canned text in the skill file — "I saw you fall. Take a breath — are you okay?" — so it is spoken instantly and still works with the LLM cold, slow, or switched off. Then TTS finishes and the microphone reopens.

  • STEP 4 · Whisper-small on HTP → qnet/<room>/heard
    Whisper transcribes on the node

    Audio never leaves the board. What comes back on the wire is either a transcript or {"silence": true} when the window timed out. Silence is a result, not an error — and it advances the ladder.

  • STEP 5 · qnet/agent/llm.py → Gemma · 424–494 ms warm
    Gemma picks from the exits this phase already allows

    Only ok or escalate are on the table — the model cannot invent a third option, and the engine checks its answer before acting. A garbled transcript is classified unclear, and the rails speak a canned "Sorry, I didn't quite catch that."

  • STEP 6 · PAIN_QUESTION — canned, on the rails
    "I'm fine" is never enough on its own

    Before any session closes, one more canned line: "Any pain? Did you hit your head?" People minimise — especially to a machine — and a head strike is exactly the injury that gets talked away. Only a clear denial closes it.

  • STEP 7 · T+30s · phase escalate · on_enter → notify_contacts
    Contacts are messaged automatically

    Silence, "no", or any report of pain moves here — and the timer moves here on its own. The phase declares on_enter: [notify_contacts], so the Telegram fires the instant the phase starts, with no agent turn involved. The room says what it did: "I've messaged Sarah."

  • STEP 8 · telegram_poller.py → qnet/<room>/contact
    Matched on the rails, never by the model

    "OK" / "on my way" from a configured contact jumps the session to contact_engaged and holds the call. A 180-second backstop starts anyway — an acknowledged incident that goes quiet must never become a stranded one. "Call 911" jumps straight to the end.

  • STEP 9 · T+60s · phase call_help · on_enter → call_emergency
    Emergency services

    on_enter: [call_emergency, notify_contacts] — the call is placed and the contact is told, both automatically. Throughout, the comfort loop keeps speaking true status on a schedule so nobody is left in silence.

  • AT ANY POINT · CANCEL_RE — engine interrupt
    Cancel is one word away

    "False alarm" spoken, the dashboard banner clicked, or typed — matched as an engine interrupt before the model ever sees the transcript. Contacts get told: "✅ False alarm — no action needed."

6 · The skills behind it

None of the above is engine code. The phases, the timers, the spoken openings and the escalation wording all live in markdown files that are validated when the agent boots.

Skill fileWhat it contributes to this scenario
skills/fall.md
urgency: safety		 The four phases above — every canned opening line, both timers, the exit conditions in plain language, the on_enter actions, and the four Telegram templates. urgency: safety is what makes an incident take over the dashboard.
skills/first-aid.md Sourced guidance sentences — IFRC 2020 First Aid Guidelines, AHA 2020 Highlights, a California DSS/IHSS fall-response guide. When the person's own words name a problem the file knows, the engine speaks that topic's sentence verbatim. The model never words medical advice.
skills/responder-brief.md
mode: interrupt		 No phases and no timers. A first responder says "I'm the first responder — what happened?" and gets a spoken handover from the session log: condition first, then what was done, then where things stand. It's an interrupt, not a session, so it can never be blocked by the live incident.

Adding a fifth phase, changing 30 seconds to 20, or rewording the escalation message is a text edit — no engine code, and a typo fails when the agent boots rather than at 3am.
